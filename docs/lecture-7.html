Example: Selective Education

Note

The following example comes from Angrist and Pischke (2014, ch. 2) Mastering Metrics. The book is an excellent read and worth getting a copy of. The example is inspired by a famous paper Estimating the payoff to attending a more selective college: An application of selection on observables and unobservables (Dale and Krueger 2002). Another excellent read.

Self-selection

Suppose you want to estimate the (causal) impact of attending a selective (\(p\)-rivate) university on future earnings (\(y\))1

  • Individuals self-select into private education

    • \(\Rightarrow p\) is endogenous to factors that plausibly determine future earnings.
WarningTerminology

Take note of how we use these words: self-select, endogenous, exogenous, uncorrelated, independent, unconfounded, etc. They all have important, but slightly different meanings.

Confounding factors

Given a simple (\(s\)-hort) linear regression,2

\[ y_i = \alpha^s + \beta^s p_i + v_i \]

there are likely many confounding factors (parental education, ability, etc.) that determine both \(y\) and \(p\).

Control variables

You might control for these confounding factors by estimating a multivariate model

\[ y_i = \alpha + \beta p + \mathbf{x}_i\gamma + u_i \]

Traditional Story: Omitted Variables

‘True’ model

The ‘true’ model is given by,3

\[ y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \beta_2 x_{i2} + u_i \]

with MLR 1-4 holding.

  • \(E[\hat{\beta}_1]=\beta_1\)

  • \(\hat{\beta}_1\rightarrow_p \beta_1\) (even under MLR 4a)

Omitted variable

In place of the ‘true’ model, you estimate the ‘short’ model,

\[ y_i = \beta_0^s + \beta_1^s x_{i1} + v_i \]

which omits \(x_2\).


NoteModel Misspecification

Wooldridge (2025, 85) discusses this problem as an example of misspecification analysis. The ‘short’ model is misspecified since it excludes variables that belong in the ‘true’ model.

What then is the relationship between,

\[ \beta_1^s \overset{?}{=}\beta_1 \]


And, if you estimate the short model, what - if anything - do you learn about the parameters from the longer (‘true’) model?

Omitted variable formula

From Lecture 1, we know that \(\beta_1^s\) can be defined as,4

\[ \beta_1^s = \frac{Cov(x_{i1},y_i)}{Var(x_{i1})} \]

If we plug in \(\textcolor{blue}{y_i}\) from the ‘true’ model, then

\[ \begin{aligned} \beta_1^s =& \frac{Cov(x_{i1},\textcolor{blue}{y_i})}{Var(x_{i1})} \\ =& \frac{Cov(x_{i1},\textcolor{blue}{\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \beta_2 x_{i2} + u_i})}{Var(x_{i1})} \\ =& \underbrace{\frac{Cov(x_{i1},\textcolor{blue}{\beta_0})}{Var(x_{i1})}}_{=0} + \underbrace{\frac{Cov(x_{i1},\textcolor{blue}{\beta_1 x_{i1}})}{Var(x_{i1})}}_{=\beta_1}+ \frac{Cov(x_{i1},\textcolor{blue}{\beta_2 x_{i2}})}{Var(x_{i1})}+ \underbrace{\frac{Cov(x_{i1},\textcolor{blue}{u_i})}{Var(x_{i1})}}_{=0} \\ =& \beta_1 + \beta_2 \frac{Cov(x_{i1},x_{i2})}{Var(x_{i1})} \end{aligned} \]

OV formula

So,

\[ \beta_1^s = \beta_1+\beta_2 \delta_1 \]

where,

  • \(\beta_2\) is the parameter on \(x_2\) in the ‘true’ model; i.e. the impact of \(x_2\) on \(y\).

  • \(\delta_1 = \frac{Cov(x_{i1},x_{i2})}{Var(x_{i1})}\) is the slope parameter from a simple regression,

    \[ x_{i2} = \delta_0 + \delta_1 x_{i1} + \xi_i \]

    i.e., the impact of \(x_1\) on \(x_2\).

OV ‘bias’

Consider the (conditional) expectation of the OLS estimator of the short regression,5

\[ E[\hat{\beta}^s_1|X] = \beta_1 + \underbrace{\beta_2\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n(x_{i1}-\bar{x}_1)x_{i2}}{\sum_{i=1}^n(x_{i1}-\bar{x}_1)^2}}_\text{`bias'} \]

and6

\[ p\lim\; \hat{\beta}^s_1 = \beta_1 + \beta_2\underbrace{\frac{Cov(x_{i1},x_{i2})}{Var(x_{i1})}}_{\delta_1} \]

TipWhy ‘bias’?

Why do we call this a bias? An estimator of is biased if \(E[\hat{\beta}_j]\neq \beta_j\). We call this a bias because of the model misspecification framing. The question here is whether the misspecified model (the ‘short’ model) can be used to estimate the ‘true’ model.

Consider the long model,

\[ y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \beta_2 x_{i2} + u_i \]

If you propose a short model with the same population parameters,

\[ y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \varepsilon_i \]

then \(\varepsilon_i = \beta_2 x_{i2} + u_i\). Well, suppose you assert that \(\beta_1\) denotes the causal impact of \(x_1\) on \(y\). Then, the short model essentially asks whether you can estimate this causal relationship using a model that omits \(x_2\).

Since, the parameters are the same,

\[ Cov(x_{i1},\varepsilon_i) = \beta_2Cov(x_{i1},x_{i2}) + Cov(x_{i1},u_i) \]

Both MLR 4 and 4a fail if \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) are correlated OR \(\beta_2=0\). Implying that the OLS estimator for is otherwise biased (for \(\beta_1\)).

However, you could argue that \(\hat{\beta}^s_1\) is unbiased estimator of \(\beta_1^s\). And the real difference here is that \(\beta_1^s\) need not equal \(\beta_1\). So, the discussion actually concerns the relationship between the population parameters and not the biasedness of the estimator. This second approach better fits the modern causal identification discussion which distinguishes well identified population moments (e.g. mean difference between two groups) and causal parameters/estimands (e.g. ATE).

“This important formula is often motivated by the notion that a longer regression – one with controls–has a causal interpretation, while a shorter rgression does not. The coefficients on the variables included in the shorter regression are therefroe said to be biased. In fact, the OVB formula is a mechanical link between coefficient vectors that applies to short and long regressions whether or not the longer regression is causal.” (Angrist and Pischke 2009, 59)

Sample analogue

The same formula holds for the OLS estimator:

\[ \hat\beta_1^s = \hat\beta_1 + \hat\beta_2\hat\delta_1 \]

  • The fact that \(\hat\delta_1\) is from a regression of \(x_2\) on \(x_1\) relates to the Frisch-Waugh Theorem of Lecture 2.

  • The multivariate estimate is a simple estimate minus the estimated OVB term

    \[ \hat\beta_1 = \hat\beta_1^s - \hat\beta_2\hat\delta_1 \]

    • But this formula works for more general case of adding additional regressors.

OVB sign

\[ OVB = \beta_1^s-\beta_1 = \beta_2\delta_1 \]


Direction of OVB
\(\delta_1<0\) \(\delta_1=0\) \(\delta_1>0\)
\(\beta_2<0\) Positive Zero Negative (‘downward’)
\(\beta_2=0\) Zero Zero Zero
\(\beta_2>0\) Negative Zero Positive (‘upward’)

  • You can predict how the exclusion of a potential confounding factor may ‘bias’ your estimates.

  • Alternatively, you can predict how the inclusion of a regressor will (in expectation) alter the existing parameters in a model.

Example: Selective Education

If you consider the single control \(x_i=SAT_i\)

\[ y_i = \alpha^s + \beta^s p_i + v_i \]

versus

\[ y_i = \alpha + \beta p_i + \gamma SAT_i + u_i \]

How do you expect \(\beta\) to change, relative to \(\beta^s\)?

Limitations

  1. We can only “control” for factors which we observe in the data

    • Caveat:

      • researchers like Dale and Krueger (2002) use clever methods to control for (some) “unobservables”

      • with repeated observations we can control for some time-invariant unobservables (forthcoming).

  2. How do we know that the controls have entered the model in the correct way.

    • Can test for model specification w.r.t. included regressors

Functional Form Misspecification

Another way in which the model may be misspecified is that is excludes nonlinear functions of the included variables.

\[ y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1x_{i1} + \beta_2x_{i2} + \dots + \beta_kx_{ik} + u_i \]

If MLR 4 holds, then if you were to add nonlinear functions the regressors, they should be irrelevant.


CautionLimitations

This test cannot be used to test for other kinds of functional form misspecification such as whether the regressors should be included in log form. For this you need to look at nonnested tests (Wooldridge 2025, sec 9.1b).

RESET test

Ramsey’s (1969) Regression Equation Specification Error Test (RESET) estimates the model,7

\[ y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1x_{i1} + \beta_2x_{i2} + \dots + \beta_kx_{ik} + \delta_1 \hat{y}_i^2 + \delta_2 \hat{y}_i^3 + \delta_3 \hat{y}_i^4 + u_i \]

where,

  • \(\hat{y}_i\) is the predicted value from the original equation.

  • This regression has \(n-k-1-3 = n-k-4\) degrees of freedom.

Null

You then test the null hypothesis,

\[ H_0: \delta_1 = \delta_2 = \delta_3 = 0 \]

Options:

  • \(F\)-stat: \(\overset{a}{\sim}F_{3,n-k-4}\) under \(H_0\) (and MLR 1-5)
  • \(LM\)-stat: \(\overset{a}{\sim}\chi^2_3\) under \(H_0\) (and MLR 1-5)
  • Heteroskedastic-robust equivalents

In Stata/R

For example, we can use the RESET test to test for non-linearities in a simple Mincer equation.

Example: Selective Education

Does this mean that, if you specify

\[ y_i = \alpha + \beta p_i + \mathbf{x}_i\gamma + u_i \]

in which,

\[ \beta = E[y_i|p_i=1,\mathbf{x}_{i}] - E[y_i|p_i=0,\mathbf{x}_{i}] \]

under MLR 4, then you have identified the causal impact of selective (private) education on earnings?


Answer: not clear.

  • If \(\beta\) is a causal parameter, then yes.

  • BUT what makes \(\beta\) a causal parameter?

Another control

Consider: what if I add another control \(z_i\). The model will then give me,

\[ \beta^\ell = E[y_i|p_i=1,\mathbf{x}_{i},z_i] - E[y_i|p_i=0,\mathbf{x}_{i},z_i] \]

where using the same OV-formula logic

\[ \beta \neq\beta^\ell \]

Is this now the ‘true’ model/parameter?

Good controls?

And should anything that is correlated with \(y_i\) and \(p_i\) be included as a control?

  • The OV formula tells us how the population parameter changes when we add additional regressors.

    • Correlations seem to be the important factor.

  • So, should we add (in this example)?

    • Occupation of graduate job: should be correlated with \(y_i\) and \(p_i\).

    • What about parental income?

    • And marital status?

The OV formula does not really answer these questions.

Summary

The OV formula provides a valuable tool for understanding:

  • relationship between the short and long regression parameters

    • i.e., impact of adding additional regressors

    • gives us the bias in a model misspecified by omitted variables

BUT, doesn’t tell us

  • which covariates to add

  • how they should appear in the model (interactions, non-linear terms, etc.)

  • why the long model is ‘true’

Modern: Conditional Independence

POF

Recall, we have a framework within which we have defined a causal effect:

\[ \tau_i = y_i\small{(1)}-y_i\small{(0)} \]

  • The problem is the ITE cannot be observed or estimated.

  • We have to compare different groups of people, which introduces selection.

Selection

In Lecture 6 we derived this important decomposition:

\[ \begin{aligned} & E[y_i|w_i = 1] - E[y_i|w_i = 0]\\ =& E[y_i\small{(1)}|w_i = 1] - E[y_i\small{(0)}|w_i = 0] \\ =&\underbrace{E[y_i\small{(1)}|w_i = 1] - \textcolor{red}{E[y_i\small{(0)}|w_i = 1]}} + \underbrace{\textcolor{red}{E[y_i\small{(0)}|w_i = 1]}- E[y_i\small{(0)}|w_i = 0]} \\ =& \underbrace{E[y_i\small{(1)}-y_i\small{(0)}|w_i = 1]}_{\tau_{att}}+ \underbrace{\textcolor{red}{E[y_i\small{(0)}|w_i = 1]}- E[y_i\small{(0)}|w_i = 0]}_\text{selection} \end{aligned} \]

Unconfoundedness

We dealt with selection by exploiting the property of unconfounded assignment.8

Unconfounded assignment: assignment into the treatment is unconfounded if

\[ y_i\small{(1)},y_i\small{(0)} \perp w_i | \mathbf{x}_i \]


NoteUnconfoundedness

Most definitions of unconfoundedness include the condition on a finite set of observable covariates. You can think of the unconditional version (from Lecture 6) as a special case of this: where assignment is unconfounded independent of observables.

CIA

  • Unconfoundedness is a property of assignment

    • conditional unconfoundedness may be the result of random assignment, stratified on \(\mathbf{x}\), in a controlled experiment.

  • In the absence of controlled assignment, unconfoundedness becomes an assumption

    • often referred to as the Conditional Independence Assumption (CIA)

    • as if treatment is randomly assigned, conditional on \(\mathbf{x_i}\).

Selection on observables

CIA tells you,

\[ E[y_i\small{(0)}|w_i = 1,\textcolor{blue}{\mathbf{x}_i}]= E[y_i\small{(0)}|w_i = 0,\textcolor{blue}{\mathbf{x}_i}] = E[y_i\small{(0)}|\textcolor{blue}{\mathbf{x}_i}] \]

which means,

\[ \underbrace{\textcolor{red}{E[y_i\small{(0)}|w_i = 1,\textcolor{blue}{\mathbf{x}_i}]}- E[y_i\small{(0)}|w_i = 0,\textcolor{blue}{\mathbf{x}_i}]}_{\text{Selection}(\mathbf{x}_i)} = 0 \]

  • Conditional \(\mathbf{x}_i\), there is no selection.

    • selection on observables: YES
    • selection on unobservables: NO

In addition,

\[ E[y_i\small{(1)}|w_i = 1,\textcolor{blue}{\mathbf{x}_i}]= E[y_i\small{(1)}|w_i = 0,\textcolor{blue}{\mathbf{x}_i}] = E[y_i\small{(1)}|\textcolor{blue}{\mathbf{x}_i}] \]

Which means,

\[ \underbrace{E[y_i\small{(1)}-y_i\small{(0)}|w_i = 1,\textcolor{blue}{\mathbf{x}_i}]}_{\text{ATT}(\mathbf{x}_i)}=\underbrace{E[y_i\small{(1)}-y_i\small{(0)}|w_i = 0,\textcolor{blue}{\mathbf{x}_i}]}_{\text{ATU}(\mathbf{x}_i)}=\underbrace{E[y_i\small{(1)}-y_i\small{(0)}|\textcolor{blue}{\mathbf{x}_i}]}_{\text{ATE}(\mathbf{x}_i)} \]

These are conditional causal estimands:

  • ATE for individuals with characteristics \(\mathbf{x}_i\)
  • ATT for individuals with characteristics \(\mathbf{x}_i\)
  • ATU for individuals with characteristics \(\mathbf{x}_i\)

Conditional comparison

\[ \begin{aligned} & E[y_i|w_i = 1,\textcolor{blue}{\mathbf{x}_i}] - E[y_i|w_i = 0,\textcolor{blue}{\mathbf{x}_i}]\\ =& \underbrace{E[y_i\small{(1)}-y_i\small{(0)}|w_i = 1,\textcolor{blue}{\mathbf{x}_i}]}_{\text{ATT}(\mathbf{x}_i)}+ \underbrace{\textcolor{red}{E[y_i\small{(0)}|w_i = 1,\textcolor{blue}{\mathbf{x}_i}]}- E[y_i\small{(0)}|w_i = 0,\textcolor{blue}{\mathbf{x}_i}]}_{\text{Selection}(\mathbf{x}_i)} \end{aligned} \]


Under unconfoundedness (or CIA),

\[ E[y_i|w_i = 1,\textcolor{blue}{\mathbf{x}_i}] - E[y_i|w_i = 0,\textcolor{blue}{\mathbf{x}_i}] = \underbrace{E[y_i\small{(1)}-y_i\small{(0)}|w_i = 1,\textcolor{blue}{\mathbf{x}_i}]}_{\tau_{att}(\mathbf{x}_i)} \]

And,

\[ \underbrace{E[y_i\small{(1)}-y_i\small{(0)}|w_i = 1,\textcolor{blue}{\mathbf{x}_i}]}_{\tau_{att}(\mathbf{x}_i)} = \underbrace{E[y_i\small{(1)}-y_i\small{(0)}|\textcolor{blue}{\mathbf{x}_i}]}_{\tau_{ate}(\mathbf{x}_i)} \]


BUT \(\tau_{ate}(\mathbf{x}_i)\) (conditional) need NOT equal \(\tau_{ate}\) (unconditional).

Matching

The CIA suggests that we need to match individuals with the same \(x\)s.

  • upon matching, it is as if assignment was randomized

  • the problem is the “dimensionality” of \(\mathbf{x}_i\)

    • same gender, ethnic background, parental income, number of siblings, etc.


Approaches to matching:

  • covariate matching: make comparisons for every possible combination

  • propensity score matching: match on \(\rho(\mathbf{x}_i) = Pr(w_i=1|\mathbf{x}_i)\) (the propensity score)

Regression as matching

When we wrote down the multivariate regression model in Lecture-1,

\[ y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \beta_2x_{i2}+\beta_3 x_{i3} + u_i \]

with \(x_{1}\in\{0,1\}\), we said

\[ \beta_1 = E[y_i|\textcolor{red}{x_{i1}=1},x_{i2},x_{i3}]-E[y_i|\textcolor{blue}{x_{i1}=0},x_{i2},x_{i3}] \]

under MLR 1-4.

Linear model

Suppose (for now) TEs are homogeneous (from Wooldridge 2025, 101):9

\[ y_i = y_i(0) + \tau w_i \]

Then,

\[ E[y_i|w_i,\mathbf{x}_i] = E[y_i(0)|w_i,\mathbf{x}_i] + \tau w_i = E[y_i(0)|\mathbf{x}_i] + \tau w_i \]

where second “=” follows from CIA.

Let us assume then,

\[ E[y_i(0)|\mathbf{x}_i] = \alpha + \mathbf{x}_i\gamma \]

i.e. linear regression function

Then,

\[ E[y_i|w_i,\mathbf{x}_i] = \alpha + \tau w_i + \mathbf{x}_i\gamma \]


Then by the CEF theorem, we can write:

\[ y_i = \alpha + \tau w_i + \mathbf{x}_i\gamma + u_i \]

where \(E[u_i|w_i,\mathbf{x}_i]=0\) (i.e. MLR 4)

  • OLS is unbiased estimator for \(\tau\).

Assumptions:

Let us keep track of the assumptions we have just made:

A-1: CIA


A-2: Homogenous TEs: \(\tau_i = \tau\)


A-3: CEF of \(y(0)\) is linear (in parameters): \(E[y_i(0)|\mathbf{x}_i] = \alpha + \mathbf{x}_i\gamma\)

Control variables

So, we have a linear regression model,

\[ y_i = \alpha + \tau w_i + \mathbf{x}_i\gamma + u_i \]

  • which identifies the causal impact of \(w\) on \(y\)

  • by “controlling” for \(\mathbf{x}\)

    • the regression “matches” on \(x\)
TipCommon Support

One of the key requirements of a matching estimator is “common support” (also referred to as overlapping). Formally, this means

\[ 0<\rho(\mathbf{x}_i)<1 \]

That is, the conditional probability of treatment cannot be 0 or 1 (denoted above by the propensity score). From an experimental perspective, this is equivalent to saying at least one individual must be treated (untreated).

Without this assumption, you cannot match treated individuals with control individuals for every combinations of the \(x\)’s. So, it is a crucial ingredient for matching estimators.

Linear regression models do not impose this requirement. In fact, you could theoretically estimate the theoretical model with a complete absence of common support, leveraging the strength of the CEF (of \(y(0)\)) assumption. This assumption allows you to predict out the counterfactual for the treated group, even in the absence of common support.

This issue is discussed in Wooldridge (2025, 686, 691–92).

Good controls

So what \(x\)s should you control for?

  • Evidently, we care about \(x\)’s that determine selection (into treatment)

  • As with RCTs, the \(x\)s should not be an outcome of the “experiment” (i.e. treatment)

“Bad controls are variables that are themselves outocome variables in the notional experiment at hand. That is, bad controls might just as well be dependent variables too.” (Angrist and Pischke 2009, 64)


Note

If you are unsure whether a variable could be a good control, ask yourself: could this be an outcome of the “treatment” variable (i.e. variable of interest).

Heterogeneity

We made a strong assumption: \(\tau_i = \tau\).

  • This seems unrealistic!

  • Solution: Unrestricted Regression Adjustment (see Wooldridge 2025, sec. 7.6a) 10

\[ y_i = \alpha + \tau_{ate}w_i + w_i\cdot (\mathbf{x}_i-\bar{\mathbf{x}}) \delta + \mathbf{x}_i\gamma + v_i \]

where,

  • \(w_i\cdot (\mathbf{x}_i-\bar{\mathbf{x}})\) is an interaction of \(w_i\) with each of the \(j\) demeaned controls.

    \[ w_i\times (x_{i1}-\bar{x}_1) + w_i\times (x_{i2}-\bar{x}_2) + \dots + w_i\times (x_{ij}-\bar{x}_j) \]

Control

Can you “control” your way out of selection (and into causation)?

  • Theoretically, YES

  • Practically, in observational studies, it is often difficult to convince the reader that you observe all important variables for selection.

  • Some policy settings can be a little “cleaner”

ImportantPolicy example

Almus and Czarnitzki (2003) paper on The effects of public R&D subsidies on firms’ innovation activities: the case of Eastern Germany exploits the fact that the government awarded grants based on a score that incorporated known criteria: size, past R&D, patents, sector, etc. While they do not know the exact scoring function, they do know the covariates upon which firms should be matched.

Example: Selective Education

If you assume A-1,2,3, then the model

\[ y_i = \alpha + \tau_{ate}p_i + \mathbf{x}_i\gamma + u_i \]

DOES, indeed, identify the causal impact of selective education.

Traditional 🤝 Modern

We can “marry” these two approaches.

Note

For this section, we will do so for using the notation in the running example.

“Marriage”

We will show,

  1. For a discrete variable (of interest), the OV formula tells you about selection into “treatment”.

    \[ \text{Traditional}\Rightarrow \text{Modern} \]

  1. When comparing two groups, the selection term is given by the OVB term (under A-1,2,3)

    \[ \text{Modern}\Rightarrow \text{Traditional} \]

This “marriage” is not always so straightforward. The discrete nature of the “treatment” (private education) considered here simplifies things.

  • As always, heterogeneous TEs complicate things.

From the Traditional

In the short (simple) model

\[ y_i = \alpha^s + \beta^s p_i + u_i \]

we know that,

\[ \beta^s = E[y_i|p_i = 1]-E[y_i|p_i=0] \]

Applying POF, this is

\[ \beta^s = \underbrace{E[y_i(1)-y_i(0)|p_i = 1]}_{\tau_{att}}+\underbrace{E[y_i(0)|p_i=1]-E[y_i(0)|p_i=0]}_{Selection} \]

Substituting in the longer model (here simplified with a single \(x\)),

\[ y_i = \alpha + \beta p_i + \gamma x_i + v_i \]

you get,

\[ \begin{aligned} \beta^s =& E[\textcolor{blue}{\alpha + \beta p_i + \gamma x_i + v_i}|p_i = 1]-E[\textcolor{blue}{\alpha + \beta p_i + \gamma x_i + v_i}|p_i=0] \\ =& \underbrace{\alpha-\alpha}_{=0} + \beta + \gamma \big[E[x_i|p_i = 1]-E[x_i|p_i=0]\big] + \underbrace{\big[E[v_i|p_i = 1]-E[v_i|p_i=0]\big]}_{=0} \\ =& \beta + \gamma \big[E[x_i|p_i = 1]-E[x_i|p_i=0]\big] \end{aligned} \]

The term,

\[ E[x_i|p_i = 1]-E[x_i|p_i=0] = \frac{Cov(x_i,p_i)}{Var(p_i)} \]

is the slope parameter from a regression of \(x\) on \(P\).

  • This was \(\delta_1\) in the OV formula

This can be rewritten as,

\[ \frac{Cov(x_i,p_i)}{Var(p_i)} = \underbrace{\frac{Cov(x_i,p_i)}{\textcolor{red}{Var(x_i)}}}_{\theta_1}\times \frac{\textcolor{red}{Var(x_i)}}{Var(p_i)} \]

where \(\theta_1\) is from,

\[ p_i = \theta_0 + \theta_1 x_i + \epsilon_i \]

  • A linear probability model of selection.

  • If \(\theta_1=0\): selection is unrelated to \(x\).

The OVB formula in this setting is,

\[ OVB = \beta^s-\beta = \gamma \delta_1 = \gamma \theta_1\frac{Var(x_i)}{Var(p_i)} \]

  • If selection is unrelated to \(x\), then OVB = 0

  • Fits with the idea of selection on observables, alone.

Under A-1,2,3, outlined earlier,

\[ \begin{aligned} \beta^s =& E[y_i|p_i=1] + E[y_i|p_i=0] \\ =& \underbrace{E[y_i(1)-y_i(0)|p_i=1]}_{ATT} + \underbrace{E[y_i(0)|p_i=1]-E[y_i(0)|p_i=0]}_\text{Selection} \end{aligned} \]

Under A-3,

\[ y_i(0) = \alpha + \gamma x_i + \varepsilon_i \]

Where, given A-1 and A-3:

\[ E[\varepsilon_i|p_i,x_i]=E[\varepsilon_i|x_i]=0 \]

The selection term is therefore,

\[ \begin{aligned} &E[y_i(0)|p_i=1]-E[y_i(0)|p_i=0] \\ =& E[\alpha + \gamma x_i + \varepsilon_i|p_i=1]-E[\alpha + \gamma x_i + \varepsilon_i|p_i=0] \\ =& \underbrace{\alpha-\alpha}_{=0} + \gamma \underbrace{\big[E[x_i|p_i=1]-E[x_i|p_i=0]\big]}_{\delta_1} + \underbrace{\big[E[\varepsilon_i|p_i=1]-E[\varepsilon_i|p_i=0]\big]}_{=0\text{ by CIA}} \\ =& \gamma \delta_1 \end{aligned} \]

Since \(\beta = \tau_{att} = \tau_{ate}\) (under A-1 and A-2)

\[ \beta^s = \tau_{att} + \text{Selection} = \beta + \gamma \delta_1 \]

which is the OV formula.

  • the multivariate regression model is explicitly modelling (“controlling for”) selection, under these assumptions.

Example: Selective Education

Identification strategy

Novel identification strategy to control for factors that may normally be unobserved (i.e. omitted).

  • Selection into selective universities is based on a number of factors; some explicit (school, grades, sporting achievements), some implicit (social class, familial connections).

  • Ultimately, it might be reasonable to assume that if two individuals received offers from the same set of schools, then they had “comparable/similar” profiles.

    • Of course, you could pick holes in this assumption.

Consider the following models:

  • Unconditional

\[ y_i = \alpha^s + \beta^s p_i + u_i \]

  • Conditional on observables: SAT and individual characteristics/demographics

\[ y_i = \alpha + \beta p_i + \gamma_1 SAT_i + \mathbf{x}_i\gamma_2 + v_i \]

  • Conditional on “unobservables”: PLUS application group dummies (fixed effects)

\[ y_i = \alpha^\ell + \beta^\ell p_i + \gamma_1^\ell SAT_i + \mathbf{x}_i\gamma_2^\ell + grp_i\psi^\ell + \varepsilon_i \]

Dale & Krueger

The take home message from Dale and Krueger (2002):

  • in observational studies, where you know little about selection, it can be quite unrealistic to “control” for all important confounding factors.

  • this need not hold in policy settings - e.g. Almus and Czarnitzki (2003) - where you know the determinants of the “treatment”.

  • we need to consider other procedures to deal with this problem of selection: for example, instrumental variables and repeated observation.

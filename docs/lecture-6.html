Potential Outcomes

Neyman-Rubin Causal Model

The Potential Outcomes Framework is a key component of the Neyman-Rubin Causal Model1

  • We won’t cover all aspects of this model.

  • I will reference texts you can visit to read further

Example

Consider out example: there are two states of the world

  1. Treated parents receive information about their kids

  2. Untreated (control) parents receive no information

We can there think about an outcome (e.g. enrollment) under each state

\[ y(\text{info}) \qquad \text{or}\qquad y(\text{status-quo}) \]

We call these potential outcomes

Possible outcomes

For a discrete outcome like enrollment there are 4 possible outcomes:

  1. Enroll only with information

    \[ y(\text{info})=\textcolor{red}{\text{enroll}} \qquad \text{and}\qquad y(\text{status-quo})=\textcolor{blue}{\text{don't enroll}} \]

  2. Enroll always

    \[ y(\text{info})=\textcolor{red}{\text{enroll}} \qquad \text{and}\qquad y(\text{status-quo})=\textcolor{red}{\text{enroll}} \]

  3. Don’t enroll always

    \[ y(\text{info})=\textcolor{blue}{\text{don't enroll}} \qquad \text{and}\qquad y(\text{status-quo})=\textcolor{blue}{\text{don't enroll}} \]

  4. Enroll only without information

    \[ y(\text{info})=\textcolor{blue}{\text{don't enroll}} \qquad \text{and}\qquad y(\text{status-quo})=\textcolor{red}{\text{enroll}} \]

Causal effect

According to the Neyman-Rubin Causal Model, in:

  • scenarios (1) and (4) there has been a causal effect

  • scenarios (2) and (3) there has been NO causal effect.

Important

Notice, to say there has been a causal effect, we must observe both potential outcomes.

TipContinuous Treatment

Add quote from Imbens & Rubin

Notation

Let \(w_i \in \{0,1\}\) be an indicator of an individuals discrete treatment status.2:

  • \(y_i(1)\) be the potential outcome when treated

  • \(y_i(0)\) be the potential outcome when not treated3

TipContinuous Treatment

Observed outcome

A priori, both potential outcomes could be realised, but in the data you observe:

\[ y_i = \begin{cases}y_i(1)\quad \text{if}\; w_i=1 \\ y_i(0)\quad \text{if}\; w_i=0 \end{cases} \]

Which we can write as the linear function:

\[ y_i = y_i(0) + w_i\big(y_i(1)-y_i(0)\big) \]

Discrete treatment

ITE

The individual (unit) treatment effect (ITE) is defined as,

\[ \tau_i = y_i(1)-y_i(0) \]

However, you cannot observe (identify) or estimate the ITE

WHY?

  • it would require you to observe both potential outcomes of the same unit in the same period of time

  • “you cannot be in two places at once”

  • often referred to as the missing data problem

Average TEs

We focus on identifying and estimating average (or aggregate) TEs

  • Average Treatment Effect (ATE)

    \[ \tau_{ate} = E[\tau_i] = E\big[y_i(1)-y_i(0)\big] \]

  • Average Treatment Effect of the Treated (ATT)

    \[ \tau_{att} = E[\tau_i|w_i = 1] = E\big[y_i(1)-y_i(0)|w_i=1\big] \]

  • Average Treatment Effect of the Untreated (ATU)

    \[ \tau_{atu} = E[\tau_i|w_i = 0] = E\big[y_i(1)-y_i(0)|w_i=0\big] \]

    These are called causal estimands

ATE vs ATT

We prefer the ATE if,

  • we want to learn about the effect on the entire population;

  • i.e., mandatory exposure.

We prefer the ATT if,

  • the population includes individuals that will not take up the treatment/policy;

  • e.g., a policy that targets single mothers;

  • where the program is voluntary, so those not enrolled will not necessarily be treated;

  • the ATT is “informative about the effect of entirely eliminating the current program” (Imbens and Wooldridge, 2009, pp. 14-15).

Assignment

Fundamental problem

Suppose you treat one group of individuals, while observing another untreated. Then in expectation, you can learn about

\[ E[y_i|w_i = 1] - E[y_i|w_i = 0] \]

Substituting in \(y_i = y_i(0) + w_i\big(y_i(1)-y_i(0)\big)\)

\[ E[y_i(1)|w_i = 1] - E[y_i(0)|w_i = 0] \]

Is this equal to either the ATE, ATT, or ATU? \[ E[y_i(1)|w_i = 1] - E[y_i(0)|w_i = 0] \]

NO!

  • The conditions are not the same!

Decomposition

Let us add and subtract:

\[ \textcolor{red}{E[y_i(0)|w_i = 1]} \]

the unobserved counterfactual for the treated group.

\[ E[y_i(1)|w_i = 1] - \textcolor{red}{E[y_i(0)|w_i = 1]} + \textcolor{red}{E[y_i(0)|w_i = 1]}- E[y_i(0)|w_i = 0] \]

The expression now has two terms

$$ \[\begin{aligned} &E[y_i(1)|w_i = 1] - \textcolor{red}{E[y_i(0)|w_i = 1]} + \textcolor{red}{E[y_i(0)|w_i = 1]}- E[y_i(0)|w_i = 0] \\ =& \underbrace{E[y_i(1)-y_i(0)|w_i = 1]}_\text{ATT}+ \underbrace{\textcolor{red}{E[y_i(0)|w_i = 1]}- E[y_i(0)|w_i = 0]}_\text{selection} \end{aligned}\]

$$

:::{callout-note title=“Selection”} As alluded to earlier in Lecture 3, selection here is different to the problem of sample selection. :::

Selection

The problem of selection into treatment is fundamental to any discussion of causation.

\[ \underbrace{\textcolor{red}{E[y_i(0)|w_i = 1]}- E[y_i(0)|w_i = 0]}_\text{selection} \]

The term describes the difference between

  1. an unobserved counterfactual outcome of the treated group

    the outcome you would have observed, had the group NOT been treated

  2. the observed outcome of the untreated group

Unconfoundedness

In order to say much more, we need an assumption:

Unconfounded assignment: assignment into the treatment is unconfounded if

\[ y_i(1),y_i(0) \perp w_i \]

And if, assignment is randomly assigned, then it is unconfounded.

Note

Some people equate the assumption of uncounfedness with randomization. However, they are better thought of as two separate things: unconfoundedness an important underlying assumption, while randomization is an assignment mechanism that gives rise to unconfounded assignment.

WarningUnconfounded vs exogenous

Unconfounded assignment

  1. \(E[y_i(0)|w_i=1] = E[y_i(0)|w_i=0] = E[y_i(0)]\)

\[ \Rightarrow \underbrace{\textcolor{red}{E[y_i(0)|w_i = 1]}- E[y_i(0)|w_i = 0]}_\text{selection} = 0 \]

  1. \(\underbrace{E[y_i(1)-y_i(0)|w_i = 1]}_\text{ATT}=\underbrace{E[y_i(1)-y_i(0)|w_i = 0]}_\text{ATU}=\underbrace{E[y_i(1)-y_i(0)}_\text{ATE}\)

If assignment is unconfounded, then

\[ E[y_i|w_i = 1] - E[y_i|w_i = 0] = \tau_{ate} \]

We therefore refer to unconfoundedness as the identifying assumption.

  • Without unconfoundness we cannot learn about the ATE (or ATT) from the difference between two groups.

Balance tests

Estimation

Estimator

Under unconfoundedness, the ATE has a simple difference between means (SDM) estimator (Wooldridge 2025, sec. 2.7a)

\[ \hat{\tau} = \bar{y}_1 - \bar{y}_0 \]

where,

$$ {y}1 = {}y_i{y}0 ={}y_i

$$

and \(n_1\) (\(n_0\)) is number of treated (control) units

Variance

The variance of this estimator is

\[ Var(\hat{\tau}) = \frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{\sigma_0^2}{n_0} \]

Which can be estimated by,4

\[ \widehat{Var}(\hat{\tau}) = \frac{\hat{\sigma}_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{\hat{\sigma}_0^2}{n_0} \]

where \(\hat{\sigma}_j^2 = \frac{1}{n_j-1}\sum_{i: w_i=j} (y_i-\bar{y}_j)^2\) for \(j=0,1\).

Homogeneous vs heterogenous TEs

So far, we have said nothing about whether \(\tau_i\) varies at the individual level. If TEs are homogeneous:

\[ \tau_i=y_i(1)-y_i(0)=\tau \;\forall\; i \]

Then,

\[ y_i = y_i(0) + w_i(y_i(1)-y_i(0)) = y_i(0) + w_i\tau \]

And,

\[ \sigma_0^2 = \sigma_1^2 = \sigma^2 \]

Homogenous TEs

If TEs homogenous are homogeneous

\[ Var(\hat{\tau}) = \frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{\sigma_0^2}{n_0} = \sigma^2\bigg(\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_0}\bigg) \]

Which can be estimated as,

\[ \hat{\sigma}^2 = \frac{1}{n-2}\big(\hat\sigma_1^2(n_1-1) + \hat\sigma_0^2(n_0-1)\big) \]

We call this the constant variance estimator.

Distribution

Note, we do not know the distribution of \(y_i(0)\) or \(y_i(1)\)!

  • In applied work, researchers prefer not to make an assumption

  • Can use CLT to show asymptotic distribution is normal

  • In practice, use \(t\)-distribution to be more conservative

Linear Model

Dummy regressor

We know that for a discrete \(x_i\in\{0,\}\),

\[ y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + u_i \]

\[ \beta_1 = E[y_i|x_i=1]-E[y_i|x_i=0] \]

OLS

Moreover, we should in Lecture 28 that the OLS estimator for \(\beta_1\) is just,

\[ \hat{\beta}_1 = \bar{y}_1 - \bar{y}_0 \]

Translation of POF

We translate the POF to a linear regression model,5

$$ \[\begin{aligned} y_i =& y_i(0) + w_i(y_i(1)-y_i(0)) \\ =& y_i(0) + \tau_i w_i \\ =& \textcolor{blue}{E[y_i(0)]} + \tau_i w_i + y_i(0)-\textcolor{blue}{E[y_i(0)]} \\ =& \textcolor{blue}{E[y_i(0)]} + \textcolor{red}{\tau_{ate}} w_i + \underbrace{y_i(0)-\textcolor{blue}{E[y_i(0)]} + (\tau_i-\textcolor{red}{\tau_{ate}})w_i}_{u_i} \\ =& \alpha_0 + \textcolor{red}{\tau_{ate}}w_i + u_i \end{aligned}\]

$$

Consider the error term:

\[ \begin{aligned} u_i =& y_i(0)-\textcolor{blue}{E[y_i(0)]} + (\tau_i-\textcolor{red}{\tau_{ate}})w_i \\ =& y_i(0)-\textcolor{blue}{E[y_i(0)]} + (y_i(1)-y_i(0)-\textcolor{red}{E[y_i(1)-y_i(0)]})w_i \\ \end{aligned} \]

  1. \(E[u_i|w_i=0]\)

    \[ E[u_i|w_i=0] = E\big[y_i(0)-\textcolor{blue}{E[y_i(0)]}\big|w_i=0\big] = E[y_i(0)|w_i=0]-\textcolor{blue}{E[y_i(0)]} \]

If assignment is unconfounded:

\[ E[y_i(0)|w_i=0]-\textcolor{blue}{E[y_i(0)]} = E[y_i(0)]-\textcolor{blue}{E[y_i(0)]} = 0 \]

  1. \(E[u_i|w_i=1]\)

    \[ \begin{aligned} E[u_i|w_i=1] =& E\big[y_i(0)-\textcolor{blue}{E[y_i(0)]} + (y_i(1)-y_i(0)-\textcolor{red}{E[y_i(1)-y_i(0)]})\big|w_i=1\big] \\ =& E\big[y_i(0)-\textcolor{blue}{E[y_i(0)]} + y_i(1)-y_i(0)-\textcolor{red}{E[y_i(1)]}+\textcolor{red}{E[y_i(0)]}\big|w_i=1\big] \\ =& E\big[y_i(1)-\textcolor{red}{E[y_i(1)]}\big|w_i=1\big] \\ =& E[y_i(1)|w_i=1] -\textcolor{red}{E[y_i(1)]} \end{aligned} \]

If assignment is unconfounded:

\[ E[y_i(1)|w_i=1] -\textcolor{red}{E[y_i(1)]} = E[y_i(1)]-\textcolor{red}{E[y_i(1)]} = 0 \]

Thus, under unconfoundedness we can write,

\[ y_i = \alpha_0 + \tau_{ate}w_i + u_i \]

where,

  • slope parameter on \(w_i\) identifies the ATE
  • the error term fullfils MLR 4
  • OLS estimator is unbiased

Important

If given the model \(y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + u_i\) with \(x_i = \{0,1\}\), then it is trivial to say that \(E[u_i|x_i]=0\). This is because we can write the CEF of \(y\) given \(x\) as a linear function:

\[ E[y_i|x_i] = \underbrace{E[y_i|x_i=0]}_{\beta_0} + \underbrace{\big(E[y_i|x_i=1]-E[y_i|x_i=0]\big)}_{\beta_1}x_i \]

All this means is that we can identify the mean of two groups. It does NOT mean that \(\beta_1 = E[y_i|x_i=1]-E[y_i|x_i=0]\) is equal to the ATE. For that, you need unconfoundedness.

Consider the case where \(x_i = \mathbf{1}\{\text{``female''}\}\). Then \(\beta_1\) tells you the mean difference between female and male observations. Would you describe this as the ATE of being female?

Error variance

We have show that under unconfoundedness \(E[u_i |w_i]=0\). What about \(Var(u_i|w_i)=E[u_i^2|w_i]\)?

  • Suppose TEs are homogenous: \(\tau_i = \tau = \tau_{ate}\;\forall\;i\)

    \[ u_i = y_i(0)-\textcolor{blue}{E[y_i(0)]} + \underbrace{(\tau_i-\textcolor{red}{\tau_{ate}})}_{=0}w_i = y_i(0)-\textcolor{blue}{E[y_i(0)]} \]

Then,

\[ E[u_i^2|w_i] = E[(y_i(0)-\textcolor{blue}{E[y_i(0)]})^2|w_i] = E[(y_i(0)-\textcolor{blue}{E[y_i(0)]})^2] = \sigma^2_0 = \sigma^2 \]

under unconfoundedness.

  • homogeneity \(\rightarrow\) homoskedastic error variance

  • If TEs are heterogenous:

\[ E[u_i^2|w_i] = \begin{cases} E[(y_i(0)-\textcolor{blue}{E[y_i(0)]})^2] = \sigma^2_0 \quad \text{if}\;w_i=0 \\ E[(y_i(1)-\textcolor{red}{E[y_i(1)]})^2] = \sigma^2_1 \quad \text{if}\;w_i=1 \\ \end{cases} \]
- heterogeneity \(\rightarrow\) heteroskedastic error variance

Variance

We know from Lecture 3 that the (conditional) variance of the OLS estimator for \(\tau_{ate}\) is,

\[ \widehat{Var}(\hat{\tau}_{ols}|W) = \frac{\hat{\sigma}^2_{ols}}{SST_w} \]

under MLR 5 (i.e., homogeneous TEs). Where,

\[ \hat{\sigma}^2_{ols} = \frac{1}{n-2}\sum_{i=1}^n \hat{u}_i^2 = \frac{1}{n-2}\big(\hat\sigma_1^2(n_1-1) + \hat\sigma_0^2(n_0-1)\big) \]

In the denominator is

\[ \begin{aligned} SST_w =& \sum_{i=1}^n(w_i-\bar{w})^2 \\ =& \sum_{i:w_i=1}(1-\bar{w})^2 + \sum_{i:w_i=0}(0-\bar{w})^2 \\ =& n_1(1-2\bar{w} + \bar{w}^2) + n_0 \bar{w}^2 \\ =& n_1(1-2\bar{w}) + n\bar{w}^2 \\ =& \underbrace{n\bar{w}}_{=n_1}(1-2\bar{w}) + n\bar{w}^2 \\ =& n\bar{w}(1-\bar{w}) \end{aligned} \]

This formula is quadratic in \(\bar{w}\) - the share of the sample treated.

  • At \(\bar{w}=0.5\), \(SST_w\) is maximized and \(Var(\hat{\tau}^{OLS}|W)\) is minimized.

What if MLR 5 (i.e. heterogeneous TEs) does not hold.

  • The White (1980) heteroskedastic-consistent estimator from Lecture 5 was,

\[ \widehat{Var}(\hat{\tau}_{ols}|W) = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(w_{i}-\bar{w})^2\hat{u}_i^2}{\text{SST}_w^2} \]

It can be shown (we will not) that,

\[ \widehat{Var}(\hat{\tau}_{ols}|W) \neq \frac{\hat{\sigma}_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{\hat{\sigma}_0^2}{n_0} \]

the Neyman estimator. The Neyman estimator is numerically equivalent to HC2.

  • in practice, all HC0-HC3 estimators are asymptotically consistent.
  • HC3 is recommended for smaller samples.
Tip

You can use the same trick of splitting the summation to show,

\[ \begin{aligned} \widehat{Var}(\hat{\tau}_{ols}|W) =& \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(w_{i}-\bar{w})^2\hat{u}_i^2}{\text{SST}_w^2} \\ =& \frac{\sum_{i:w_i=1}^{n}(1-\bar{w})^2(y_i-\hat{\alpha}_{ols}-\hat{\tau}_{ols})^2+\sum_{i:w_i=0}^{n}(-\bar{w})^2(y_i-\hat{\alpha}_{ols})^2}{\text{SST}_w^2} \\ =& \frac{(1-\bar{w})^2\sum_{i:w_i=1}^{n}(y_i-\hat{\alpha}_{ols}-\hat{\tau}_{ols})^2+\bar{w}^2\sum_{i:w_i=0}^{n}(y_i-\hat{\alpha}_{ols})^2}{\text{SST}_w^2} \\ =& \frac{(1-\bar{w})^2(n_1-1)\hat\sigma_1^2+\bar{w}^2(n_0-1)\hat\sigma_0^2}{(n\bar{w}(1-\bar{w}))^2} \\ =& \frac{n_1-1}{n_1^2}\hat\sigma_1^2+\frac{n_0-1}{n_0^2}\hat\sigma_0^2 \end{aligned} \]

And HC1 (Stata’s robust default) will be,

\[ \frac{n}{n-2}\times \Bigg[\frac{n_1-1}{n_1^2}\hat\sigma_1^2+\frac{n_0-1}{n_0^2}\hat\sigma_0^2\Bigg] \]

It is evident that for \(n\) large, these estimators will be very similar to Neyman’s.

Regression Adjustment

Purpose

The variance of the estimator is (assuming homogeneous TEs),

\[ Var(\hat{\tau}_{ols}|W) = \frac{Var(u_i|w_i)}{SST_w} = \frac{\sigma^2_u}{SST_w} \]

where \(u_i\) is from the model,

\[ y_i = \alpha_0 + \tau_{ate}w_i + u_i \]

  • The only variation in \(y\) explained by this model is the difference between treatment and control.
  • Will result in high variance and low-powered test.

Covariates

Suppose,

\[ u_i = \gamma x_i + v_i \]

where \(x\) is a regressor measured before assignment6

  • likely explain part of the outcome (i.e. \(\gamma\neq 0\))

  • commonly referred to as baseline covariates in RCTs

Regression adjustment

Instead of the simple regression, we estimate

\[ y_i = \alpha_0 + \tau_{ate}w_i + \gamma x_i + v_i \]

  • Wooldridge refers to this as the Restricted Regression Adjustment (RRA) (~ Wooldridge 2025, 257)

Variance

\[ Var(\hat{\tau}_{rra}|W,X) = \frac{Var(v_i|w_i,x_i)}{SSR_w} = \frac{\sigma^2_v}{SSR_w} \]

where, \(SSR_w=SST_w (1-R_w^2)\)

  • \(R^2_w\) is the amount of variance in assignment explained by the covariates. This should be low.

  • While \(R^2_w\neq 0\) in the finite sample, \(R^2_w\rightarrow 0\) as \(n\rightarrow \infty\)

  • So, \(SSR_w\approx SST_w\).

Power

The improvement in power comes from the fact that,

\[ Var(u_i|w_i) = Var(\gamma_ix_i + v_i|w_i) = \underbrace{\gamma^2 Var(x_i)}_{>0} + Var(v_i|w_i,x_i) > \sigma^2_v \]

assuming \(Cov(x_i,v_i|w_i)=0\).

Heterogeneity

This simple story is somewhat complicated by heterogeneity:

  • heterogeneity is sits in the error term

  • heterogeneity is likely related to \(x\)

  • heterogeneity (that depends on \(x\)) introduces small-sample bias in models of the form:

    \[ y_i = \alpha_0 + \tau_{ate}w_i + \gamma x_i + v_i \]

  • OLS remains consistent (for the ATE), despite this small-sample bias

URA

To avoid this small-sample bias, you can do Unrestricted Regression Adjustment (URA) (Wooldridge 2025, 257, 259).

\[ y_i = \alpha_0 + \tau_{ate}w_i + \gamma x_i + \eta w_i\times (x_i-\bar{x}) + \varepsilon_i \]

  • includes an interaction between the treatment indicator and the covariate(s)

  • the interactions include demeaned covariate(s)

    • this changes the interpretation of \(\eta\) (see Wooldridge 2025, sec. 6.2d)

    • ensures that the coefficient on \(w\) remains the ATE

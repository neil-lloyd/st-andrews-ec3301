RCT Power Calculation: Gennetian et al. (2024)

This question takes inspiration from Gennetian et al. (2024). Available online from www.nature.com. The study evaluates how low income households use unconditional cash transfers to investment in their children’s education. We will use this study to demonstrate the significance of power calculations.

While discussing their research design, the authors write:

“Striking a balance between statistical power and project costs, 40% of the recruited sample within each site was randomized to receive $333 monthly cash gifts and 60% to receive $20 monthly cash gifts. With an enrolled sample of \(n=1,000\) mother–infant dyads, and accounting for a predicted 20% attrition over longer-term follow-ups, the anticipated sample size of 800 dyads during subsequent waves of data collection is estimated to provide 80% power to detect a 0.207 s.d. impact at \(P<0.05\) in a two-tailed test on the family investment and related family process outcomes examined here. Data collectors were blinded to assignment of treatment status.” (p. 1523)


  1. Write down the formula for the variance of the simple difference between means (SDM) \(\hat\tau\) estimator, assuming a homogeneous treatment effect.

\[ Var(\hat{\tau}) = \sigma^2 \bigg(\frac{1}{n_0} + \frac{1}{n_1}\bigg) \]


  1. Solve for \(sd(\hat\tau)\) as a function of \(\sigma\), assuming \(n = 800\) and \(\bar{w} = 0.4\) (the parameters specified above).

\[ Var(\hat{\tau}) = \sigma^2 \bigg(\frac{1}{n_0} + \frac{1}{n_1}\bigg) = \sigma^2 \bigg(\frac{1}{n\bar{w}} + \frac{1}{n(1-\bar{w})}\bigg) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n\bar{w}(1-\bar{w})} \]

Thus,

\[ sd(\hat\tau) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{800\times 0.4\times 0.6}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{192}} \]

where \(\sigma\) is the unobserved s.d. of \(y\).


  1. What is the distribution of the test-statistic \(\hat{\tau}/sd(\hat\tau)\) under the hull hypothesis \(H_0: \tau=0\)?

We do not know the finite sample of this distribution of this test static. However, we know that by CLT the asymptotic distribution will be normal. For slightly more conservative test, we can use the \(t\)-distribution.


  1. Solve for power function the two-sided test: \(H_0: \tau=0\) against \(H_1: \tau\neq0\) (\(\alpha = 0.05\)). That is, expand on,

    \[ Pr(\text{Reject}\; H_0| \tau = \kappa) \]

    Use the critical values from a \(N(0,1)\) distribution for the rejection rule. The answer should include the expression \(\kappa/\sigma\)

\[ \begin{aligned} & Pr(\text{Reject}\; H_0| \tau = \kappa) \\ =& Pr\bigg(\frac{\hat{\tau}}{sd}<\text{z}_{0.025}\bigg|\tau = \kappa\bigg) + Pr\bigg(\frac{\hat{\tau}}{sd}>\text{z}_{0.975}\bigg|\tau = \kappa\bigg) \\ =& Pr\bigg(\frac{\hat{\tau}-\kappa}{sd}<\text{z}_{0.025}-\frac{\kappa}{sd}\bigg|\tau = \kappa\bigg) + Pr\bigg(\frac{\hat{\tau}-\kappa}{sd}>\text{z}_{0.975}-\frac{\kappa}{sd}\bigg|\tau = \kappa\bigg) \\ =& Pr\bigg(\frac{\hat{\tau}-\kappa}{sd}<\text{z}_{0.025}-\frac{\kappa}{\sigma}\times \sqrt{192}\bigg|\tau = \kappa\bigg) + Pr\bigg(\frac{\hat{\tau}-\kappa}{sd}>\text{z}_{0.975}-\frac{\kappa}{\sigma}\times \sqrt{192}\bigg|\tau = \kappa\bigg) \end{aligned} \]


  1. The authors compute the power of the test for \(\kappa/\sigma = 0.207\): “to detect a 0.207 s.d. impact”. Using the above expression, compute the power assuming

    \[ \frac{\hat{\tau}-\kappa}{sd(\hat{\tau})}\overset{a}{\sim} N(0,1) \] Your answer will not be exactly 80%, but should be close.

\[ \begin{aligned} & Pr(\text{Reject}\; H_0| \tau = \kappa) \\ \approx& Pr(z<\text{z}_{0.025}-0.207\times \sqrt{192}|\tau = \kappa) + Pr(z>\text{z}_{0.975}-0.207\times \sqrt{192}|\tau = \kappa) \\ =& 0.82 \end{aligned} \]

dis normal(invnormal(0.025)-0.207*sqrt(192)) + 1-normal(invnormal(0.975)-0.207*sqrt(192))
.81814403

Suppose the authors had sufficient budget to treat half the sample. Then \(n\bar{w}(1-\bar{w})=200\) and the power would increase to:

dis normal(invnormal(0.025)-0.207*sqrt(200)) + 1-normal(invnormal(0.975)-0.207*sqrt(200))
.83334297

Just a 1% increase in power.

If they were to reduce the number of treated individuals from \(n_1=320\) (at 40%) to \(260\) (60 fewer), they would save about $20,000. This is enough to include an additional 1000 control units (at $20 each). Doing so would increase power by a factor of,

\[ n\bar{w}(1-\bar{w}) = \bigg(\frac{1}{n_0} + \frac{1}{n_1}\bigg)^{-1} = \bigg(\frac{1}{1480} + \frac{1}{260}\bigg)^{-1} \approx 221 \]

dis normal(invnormal(0.025)-0.207*sqrt(220)) + 1-normal(invnormal(0.975)-0.207*sqrt(220))
.86657445

A bigger increase of 5%. Of course, we are ignoring the additional cost of surveying 1000 more individuals, which is significant.


  1. On p. 1524, the authors write:

“Estimates for each follow-up wave following pre-registration protocols are derived by the following straightforward model: \[ Y = Z\pi + X\beta + \varepsilon \] where \(Y\) is the outcome of interest for mother or the household and \(\varepsilon\) is the error term. The model includes the following baseline covariates (\(X\)) with the goal of improving precision in the estimate: mother’s characteristics (mother’s age, maximum education level attained, race and ethnicity, marital status, general health, an indicator of maternal depressive symptoms and cigarettes and alcohol consumption during pregnancy), household characteristics (number of children born to mother, number of adults in the household, father living with the mother, household income and household net worth), baby’s birth characteristics (weight at birth and gestational age), a site-based fixed effect and an indicator for the switch from in-person to phone interviews.”

Describe the econometric rationale for adding these baseline covariates in a linear regression model.

This an example of Restricted Regression Adjustment (RRA). The rationale is to lower the variance of the estimator by explaining more of the variance in the outcome. This works well in RCTs because assignment into treatment is typically independent of baseline covariates. The caviate here is stratified experiments.


  1. The original working paper version of this paper shows the conditional and unconditional estimates in Tables 1-5: www.nber.org. Why are the estimated SEs of the conditional ATE point estimates (column 4) not all smaller than the unconditional ones (column 3)?

You can show that the asymptotic variance of the RRA estimator is lower than the simple OLS estimator. However, the finite sample variance may not be bigger because of the non-zero correlation between assignment and baseline covariates in the realized data.

Proxy & IV Example: Acemoglu, Johnson & Robinson (2001)

This question concerns Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson (2001) “The Colonial Origins of Comparative Development: An Empirical Investigation”. Available online from www.aeaweb.org. The goal of the paper is to understand the (causal) relationship between development and institutions. The consider a range of institutions, by one key measure is an index of protection against expropriation. The authors estimate the following linear model using OLS (p. 1378; see results in Table 2).

\[ \log y_i = \mu + \alpha R_i + \mathbf{X}_i'\gamma + \varepsilon_i \]

where

  • \(y_i\) is income per capita,

  • \(R_i\) is measure of protection against expropriation. It is measured on a scale from 0 to 10, with 0 indicating no protection.

  • \(\mathbf{X}_i\) a vector of control variables.


  1. What are the control variables (covariates) included in this regression.

The covariates include: latitude of the country, regional dummy variable variables (Asia, Africa, and Other).


  1. If we think of these variables as proxy variables, what would they be proxy for? Think about the unobserved factors which may confound the relationship between development and institutions.

These geographical controls are being used to control for geographical (climate, disease, ruggedness, coastline, etc.) as well as cultural (social) determinants of development that vary across space.


  1. Interpret coefficient of interest in column (2) of Table 2. The base sample includes “64 countries that were ex-colonies and for which we have settler mortality, protection against expropriation risk, and GDP data” (p. 1377).

An increase in the index of 1 unit increases predicted output by 68%, among ex-colonies and holding other factors fixed.

dis (exp(0.52)-1)*100
68.202765

This is a large point estimate. The mean of \(R\) is 6.5 with a standard deviation of 1.5 (Table 1). Thus, a one standard deviation change in expropriation risk is associated with more than 100% increase in GDP per capita. In Table A2, Nigeria has a \(R=5.55\) compared with Chile’s 7.82, while their (log) of GDP per capita is 6.81 and 9.35, respectively. This point estimate suggests that the difference in the countries’ \(R\) of 2.27 would predict a difference in log GDP per capita of \(2.27\times 0.68 = 1.18\). This is just under half the difference in their log GDP per capita (=2.54).


  1. Comparing the coefficient estimates of columns (2) and (6), is specification (2) upward or downward biased due to omitted variables? In particular, consider the relationship between climate, which is proxied for by latitude. What do the results suggest is the relationship bewteen climate and institutions?

“Many social scientists, including Montesquieu [1784] (1989), Diamond (1997), and Sachs and coauthors, have argued for a direct effect of climate on performance, and Gallup et al. (1998) and Hall and Jones (1999) document the correlation between distance from the equator and economic performance. To control for this, in columns (3)–(6), we add latitude as a regressor (we follow the literature in using the absolute value measure of latitude, i.e., distance from the equator, scaled between 0 and 1).” (p. 1379)

After controlling for geographical variables, the estimated cofficient drops to 0.41. This suggests that the simple model is upward biased. The coefficient on latitude is positive, suggesting that the relationshp between instutitions and latitude is positive too. This means that, on average, ex-colonies further from the equator have stronger institutions.


  1. Explain why the authors believe \(R_i\) to be endogenous, even conditional on \(\mathbf{X}\)?

These remain relatively “crude” controls. The controls account for geographical confounding factors - like climate and cultural differences that matter for development - but it is too strong to say that conditional on geography it is as if institutions are randomly assigned. In addition, we need to account for the potential reverse causal relationship between development and institutions. This cannot be resolved using controls.


  1. What do the authors propose as an instrument for \(R_i\)? Under what assumptions is this a valid instrument? [Hint: see p. 1383]

The proposed, excluded instrument is the log of settler mortality; a historical variable from the colonial period. The conditions needed are:

\[ Cov(\log M_i,\varepsilon_i)=0\qquad \text{and}\qquad Cov(\log M_i, R_i) \neq 0 \]


  1. Explain why this historical instrument might affect contemporary levels of institutions in an ex-colony. That is, why do we expect there to be a valid first-stage? [Hint: the authors describe a chain of causation on pp. 1370, 1383]

The causal relationship runs between the following channels:

\[ \text{settler mortality} \Rightarrow \text{level of colonial settlement} \Rightarrow \text{colonial institutions} \Rightarrow \text{modern institutions} \]


  1. Could \(\log M_i\) have a direct effect on contemporary development, thereby violating the exclusion restriction? Consider the potential significance of \(\mathbf{X}\) here. [Hint: see p. 1371]

Historical settler mortality rates may be directly related to disease environments that matter today. First, the authors argue that historically important diseases often affected settlers more than native populations. In addition, to the extent to which modern disease environments relate to geography - for example, Malaria is a tropical disease - these are controlled for through \(\mathbf{X}\).


  1. The first-stage equation is shown below (p. 1384). Compute the F-statistic for \(H_0: \beta=0\) using column (10) from Table 3.

    \[ R_i = \zeta + \beta \log M_i + \mathbf{X}_i'\delta + \upsilon_i \]

The \(F\)-stat is the square of the \(t\)-stat:

\[ F\text{-stat} = \bigg(\frac{-0.51}{0.14}\bigg)^2 \]

  1. Comparing these results to those of Table 2, what can we conclude with respect to the bias in OLS? How does this compare with your answer to Q4.

The IV estimates are substantially larger than the OLS estimates (across all control-variable specifications). This suggests that OLS is in fact downward biased; the opposite direction to what we concluded in Q4. However, Q4 only concerned the OV bias from the specific controls considered here. The bias from other OVs and reverse causality may point in a different direction.

Are these realistic? The authors think so:

“Does the 2SLS estimatemake quantitative sense?Doesit implythat institutionaldifferences canexplainasignificant fractionof income differences across countries? Let us once again compare two”typical” countries with high and low expropriation risk, Nigeria and Chile (these countries are typical for the IV regression in the sense that they are practically on the regression line). Our 2SLS estimate, 0.94, implies that the 2.24 differences in expropriation risk between these two countries should translate into 206 log point (approximately 7-fold) difference. In practice, the presence of measurement error complicates this interpretation, because some of the difference be tween Nigeria and Chile’s expropriation index may reflect measurement error. Therefore, the 7-fold difference is an upper bound. In any case, the estimates in Table 4 imply a substantial, but not implausibly large, effect of institutional differences on income per capita.” (pp. 1386-1387)

The authors get the 206 log point difference from \(0.94\times 2.24=2.1056\) (difference is likely due to rounding in reported estimates). If you then compute,

dis exp(2.06)-1
6.8459698

This tells you that Chile’s GDP per capita is predicted to be \(\approx 700\%\) larger than Nigeria’s. The authors suggest this is a 7-fold difference, which is evidently referring to the gap between the two countries and not the ratio of their GDP per capita. These estimates put the ratio of the two GDPs at closer to \(\exp(2.06)\approx 8\).


  1. Suppose \(Cov(\log M_i,\varepsilon_i)\neq 0\). Explain why the IV estimates may themselves be biased in a different direction to OLS.

From Lecture 8:

\[ p\lim \hat\alpha_{ols} = \alpha + Corr(R,\varepsilon)\times \frac{\sigma_{\varepsilon}}{\sigma_R} \]

\[ p\lim \hat\alpha_{iv} = \alpha + \frac{Corr(\log M,\varepsilon)}{Corr(\log M,R)}\times \frac{\sigma_{\varepsilon}}{\sigma_R} \]

Since \(Corr(\log M,R)<0\) (from first-stage), the (inconsitency) bias of IV may be actually be positive if \(Corr(\log M,\varepsilon)<0\). This would mean that ex-colonies with higher settler mortality are negatively correlated with unobserved factors that positively affect development today. For example, if historical disease environments (like Malaria) still matter today for development.

Recent IV Examples

Below you will find a list of recent NBER working papers that make use of an instrumental variable identification strategy. For each paper, try to find: (1) the endogenous variable; (2) the excluded instrument(s); (3) the source of exogenous variation.


  1. Angrist et al. (2025) available online www.nber.org.

  1. Taking Menopause Hormone Treatment (MHT).

  2. Assignment in the Women’s Health Initiative (WHI) clinical trials.

  3. Randomization


  1. Stevenson et al. (2023) available online www.nber.org. [Hint: p. 12]

  1. Noncarceral conviction of criminals in Virginia

  2. The predicted rate of noncarceral convinction for the assigned judge

  3. Quazi-random assignment of judges to cases within the judicial system


  1. Grossman, Tello-Trillo, and Willage (2022) available online www.nber.org. [Hint: p. 15]

  1. The public health insurance status of a mother’s \(J\) children.

  2. Simulated eligibility of the mother’s children based on their age, year, and state at point of observation. This aggregated across children for each mother. There is a second version with aggregates years of eligibility for each mother based on past and present eligibility.

  3. The model includes mother FEs, state-times-year(time) FEs, as well as a dummy for each possible YOB of a child. These are all the factors that determine eligibility and closely predict eligibility of a mother’s children. The authors exploit the fact that eliibility rules change over time. Thus, a given mother’s children may gain/lose eligibility over time due to policy variation alone (and not just changes in eligibility criteria like age). This policy variation is the identifying variation of the paper (see Figure 1).

Note, this paper does not actually impliement a 2SLS IV estimation. This is because the endogenous variable - the number of insured children in household - is not actually observed in the data.


  1. Dettling and Kearney (2025) available online www.nber.org. [Hint: p. 16-21]

  1. The number of either Federal Housing Administration or Veterans Association (or combined) mortgages issued in a state and year.

  2. An interaction of the spread in period \(t-1\) and the number of lenders in state \(s\). The spread in the difference between interest of FHA bonds and Aaa corporate bonds in that year.

  3. “The rigidity of the government-backed mortgage interest rates led to annual supply-side variation in institutions’ willingness to issue these mortgages.” (p. 19) This time variation is then translated to the state-year level through an interaction with the number of state-level lenders.


  1. Caiumi and Peri (2024) available online www.nber.org. [Hint: p. 26-28]

  1. The log of foreign-born employment in a given education-experience cell

  2. The predicted foreign-born population in each education-experience cell based on historic migration flows.

  3. Historical persistence of source-country migration flows that are assumed to be unrelated to cell-specific demand factors.

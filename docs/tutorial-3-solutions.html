Application: Linear Probability Model

This question makes use of data from the Global Entrepreneurship Monitor. The analysis uses a 10% random sample of the publicly-available 2022 GEM survey.

Consider the following linear probability model of enterpreneurial intent. The outcome variable futsup is a discrete variable: \(=1\) if answered yes to “Are you, alone or with others, expecting to start a new business, including any type of self-employment, within the next three years?”; and \(=0\) if answered no.

\[ futsup_i = \alpha + \mathbf{perceptions}_i\beta + \mathbf{x}_i\gamma + u_i \]

There are four \(\mathbf{perceptions}_i\) variables:1

  • knowent: Do you know someone personally who started a business in the past 2 years?

  • opport: In the next six months, will there be good opportunities for starting a business in the area where you live?

  • suskill: Do you have the knowledge, skill and experience required to start a new business?

  • fearfail: Would fear of failure prevent you from starting a business?

With the exception of knowent, all responses are provided on a agreement scale: 1 “Strongly disagree”; 2 “Somewhat disagree”; 3 “Neither agree nor disagree”; 4 “Somewhat agree”; and 5 “Strongly agree”. For the pusposes of this exercise, the variables have been discretized: \(=1\) if answers 4 or 5; \(=0\) otherwise (called AGREEopport, AGREEsuskill, AGREEfearfail). The variable knowent has been discretized too: \(=1\) if knows 2 or more; \(=0\) if knows less than two people (called YESknowent).


  1. Under MLR 1-4, provide an interpretation of the \(E[futsup_i|\mathbf{perceptions}_i,\mathbf{x}_i]\) for this linear probability model. Hint: take a look at Wooldridge (2025, sec. 7.5).

Since the outcome is a binary \(\{0,1\}\),

\[ E[futsup_i|\mathbf{perceptions}_i,\mathbf{x}_i] = Pr(futsup_i=1|\mathbf{perceptions}_i,\mathbf{x}_i) \]

The CEF tells you the conditional probability of entrepreneurial intention.


  1. Provide an interpretation of the coefficient on YESknownent in the model (under MLR 1-4). Hint: the extra material on Interpreting Linear Models includes examples of linear probability models.

Under MLR1-4, \(\beta_{YESknowent}\) is increase in the probability of demonstrating an intent to be an entrepreneur among those who know 2 or more entrepreneurs versus those who do not; holding other factors constant.

  1. Is it reasonable to assume that the error term has a constant (conditional) variance (MLR 5) and a (conditional) standard normal distribution (MLR 6) in a linear probability model? Is this a problem for the Chow test in Q.9? And, if so, is there a solution? Hint: consider the material in Wooldridge (2025, secs. 7.5, 8.5).

The error term cannot be standard normal. Consider, \(y_i\in\{0,1\}\). Therefore, error can only take on two values conditional on the value of the regressors,

\[ u_i = \begin{cases} 1-(\alpha + \mathbf{perceptions}_i\beta + \mathbf{x}_i\gamma) \\ -(\alpha + \mathbf{perceptions}_i\beta + \mathbf{x}_i\gamma) \end{cases} \]

The error term is has a binomial distribution with a heteroskedastic variance (Wooldridge 2025). Despite this, the OLS estimator will still has an asymptotically normal distribution. And one can estimate heteroskedastic-consistent SEs.

However, the Chow test is invalid without MLR 5. The Chow test can be performed without MLR 6, since the test-statistic as a chi-squared asymptotic distribution (under MLR 1-5). However, without MLR 5, the approach adding up SSR across separately estimated models is invalid. The alternative is to estimate a fully interacted model and use either a heteroskedastic-robust \(F\)-test or \(LM\)-test.

Since, the finite sample distribution of these tests are not known, one would have to be confident that the sample size is sufficiently large to approximate the distribution of the test using the asymptotic distribution.

Theory TRUE/FALSE

State whether each of the following is TRUE/FALSE and provide a reason.


  1. The OLS estimator converges in distribution as follows:

\[ \hat{\beta}_j \rightarrow_d N\big(\beta_j, Var(\hat{\beta}_j)\big) \]

FALSE. The OLS estimator converges in probability to the true population parameter (under MLR 1-4 (or 4a)); while \(\sqrt{n}(\hat{\beta}_j-\beta_j)\) converges in distribution.


  1. The finite sample variance estimator converges in probability to the asymptotic variance of the OLS estimator.

\[ \frac{\sigma^2}{SSR_j} \rightarrow_p Var(\hat{\beta}_j) \]

FALSE. The following holds:

\[ \frac{\sigma^2}{n^{-1}SSR_j} \rightarrow_p Var(\hat{\beta}_j) \]


  1. \(t\)-stat \(\overset{a}{\sim}N(0,1)\) under MLR 1-5, because \(\hat{\sigma}^2\rightarrow_p \sigma^2\).

TRUE. Since \(\hat{\sigma}^2\rightarrow_p \sigma^2\),

\[ t\text{-stat} = \frac{\hat{\beta}_j-\beta_j}{\sqrt{\hat{\sigma}^2/SSR_j}} = \frac{\sqrt{n}(\hat{\beta}_j-\beta_j)}{\sqrt{\hat{\sigma}^2/(n^{-1}SSR_j)}} \]

Since,

\[ \hat{\sigma}^2/(n^{-1}SSR_j) \rightarrow_p Var(\hat{\beta}_j) \quad \text{and}\quad \sqrt{n} (\hat{\beta}_j - \beta_j) \rightarrow_d N\big(0,Var(\hat{\beta}_j)\big) \]

\[ t\text{-stat} \rightarrow_d N(0,1) \]


Consider the linear regression model for questions 4-6

\[ y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1x_{i1} + \beta_2x_{i2} + \beta_3x_{i3} + u_i \]


  1. The test statistic for \(H_0: 2\beta_1 = 3\beta_2 -5\) is,

\[ t\text{-stat} = \frac{2\hat{\beta}_1-3\beta_2-5}{\sqrt{4Var(\hat{\beta}_1)-9Var(\hat{\beta}_2)}} \]

FALSE. There is a mistake in both the numerator and denominator.

\[ t\text{-stat} = \frac{2\hat{\beta}_1-3\beta_2+5}{\sqrt{4Var(\hat{\beta}_1)+9Var(\hat{\beta}_2)-12Cov(\hat{\beta}_1,\hat{\beta}_2)}} \]


  1. The \(LM\)-stat for \(H_0: \beta_1 = \beta_2 = \beta_3 = 0\) is,

\[ LM\text{-stat} = nR^2 \]

where \(R^2\) is the from the regression of \(y\) on \(x_1,x_2,x_3\).

TRUE. The restricted model is a regression of \(y\) on a constant (with \(R^2=0\)). The residual from this model is just \(\tilde y = y-\bar{y}\). The auxiliary regression is therefore,

\[ \tilde y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1x_{i1} + \beta_2x_{i2} + \beta_3x_{i3} + v_i \]

Which has the same \(R^2\) as the original model.


  1. The restricted model for \(H_0: \beta_1 = 0.5,\; \beta_3 = 2\beta_2\) is,

\[ z_i = \gamma_0 + \gamma_2 w_i + v_i \]

where \(z_i = y_i-0.5x_{i1}\) and \(w_i = x_{i2}+2x_{i3}\).

TRUE. We arrive at the restricted model by applying the null to the model:

\[ y_i = \beta_0 + 0.5 x_{i1} + \beta_2x_{i2} + 2\beta_2 x_{i3} + u_i \]

The coefficient on \(x_{i1}\) is no longer an unknown parameter, which means we can deduct it from both sides of the equation. In addition, we can collect the common \(\beta_2\) terms. This gives us the equation.

\[ (y_i-0.5x_{i1}) = \gamma_0 + \gamma_2 (x_{i2}+2x_{i3}) + v_i \]


  1. If the error variance is heteroskedastic, OLS is unbiased.

Neither TRUE nor FALSE. Unbiasedness depends on MLR 1-4 only, not MLR 5. MLR 5 has no baring on unbiasedness.


  1. The following variance estimator has the same limit as the White formula (HC0). It is therefore heteroskedastic consistent.

\[ \frac{(\sum_{i=1}^n \hat{r}^2_{ij}\hat{u}^2_i)/(n-k-1)}{n^{-1}SSR_j^2} \]

TRUE. This formula is just the scaled version of HC0: HC1.

\[ \frac{(\sum_{i=1}^n \hat{r}^2_{ij}\hat{u}^2_i)/(n-k-1)}{n^{-1}SSR_j^2} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n \hat{r}^2_{ij}\hat{u}^2_i}{SSR_j^2}\times \frac{n}{n-k-1} \]

Since HC0 (the White formula) has the appropriate asymptotic limit and \(n/(n-k-1)\rightarrow 1\), HC1 is also heteroskedastic consistent.


  1. The following regression can be used to test for heteroskedasticity:

\[ \hat{u}^2_i = \delta_0 + \delta_1 x_{i1} + \delta_2 x_{i2} + \dots + \delta_k x_{ik} + v_i \]

TRUE. This auxiliary regression tests for heteroskedasticity related to the regressors in the model. There are however other forms of heteroskedasticity not tested by this model.

You can use either an \(F\)-stat or \(LM\)-stat for this test.


  1. The following \(LM\)-stat statistic has a \(\chi^2_k\) distribution.

\[ LM\text{-stat}=n\times \mathbf{R}_{\hat u^2}^2 \]

where \(\mathbf{R}_{\hat u^2}^2\) is the R-squared from the auxiliary regression in Q.9.

FALSE. This is only true under the null, since the \(LM\) test relies on MLR 5. In addition, we know the asymptotic distribution of this test-static, but not its finite sample distribution. Therefore, there are many settings in which this statistic is not known to have a \(\chi^2_k\) distribution.
